## Live streams are the interesting case

A recorded video will eventually get auto-captions, and often auto-translated ones. A live
stream will not — not while it is running, which is exactly when you need it. A keynote, a
results call, a government briefing, a conference track in a language you do not read:
these are live, in a browser tab, and unsubtitled at the only moment they matter.

VoxTranslate translates what is playing now. There is nothing to wait for, because it
listens to the audio rather than to a caption file that does not exist yet. See
[how to translate a YouTube live stream](/guides/translate-a-youtube-live-stream/).

## When YouTube's own captions are the better answer

Say this plainly: if the video already has decent auto-translated captions in your
language, use them. They are free, they are instant, and paying per minute for something
YouTube gives away is a bad trade.

The extension is for the cases where that fails — a live stream, a language pair YouTube
handles poorly, a video with no captions at all, or a talk you would rather **listen** to
in your own language than read while trying to follow slides.

## Reading versus listening

For a forty-minute conference talk with slides, subtitles compete with the screen for your
attention. Spoken translation does not. The original stays audible underneath at whatever
level you set, so you keep the speaker's timing, laughter and emphasis while understanding
the words.

That trade goes the other way for anything where the original audio *is* the point. Music
is the obvious one.
