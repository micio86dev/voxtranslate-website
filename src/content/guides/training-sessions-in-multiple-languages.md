Training is one of the few formats where the asynchronous option is often genuinely better than the live one — which makes it worth deciding deliberately rather than defaulting to a live session with an interpreter.

## Instruction or discussion?

**Instruction** — someone knows something, others need to learn it, and the content does not change based on who is in the room. This does not need to be live. Record it once, subtitle it in every language, and let people watch at their own pace, pausing and rewinding. That is strictly better than a live translated session for anyone working in a second language.

**Discussion** — case studies, role-play, sessions where the group's own situations are the material. This needs to be live and needs real bidirectional translation, because the value is in what participants contribute.

Most corporate training is labelled discussion and delivered as instruction. Be honest about which one you are running.

## Translate the assessment, not just the delivery

The most common and most consequential gap. Compliance training delivered in five languages and assessed with an English multiple-choice quiz is measuring English comprehension, not compliance understanding — and if a regulator ever asks, that is exactly what your records will show.

If people sign off on having understood something, they must be assessed in a language they actually understood it in.

## Design for a second-language audience

- **Slow the transitions**, not the whole delivery
- **Put every number and name on screen**, never voice-only
- **Provide a glossary** of domain terms before the session
- **Build in checkpoints** — short exercises rather than "any questions?", which reliably produces silence
- **Give exercise instructions in writing** as well as spoken; someone who misheard the task will spend the exercise reconstructing it

## Small-group work needs planning

Breakout rooms are where multilingual training either works or collapses.

Grouping by language makes the discussion productive and the debrief harder. Mixing languages makes the debrief coherent and the discussion thin. There is no free answer — pick based on whether the value is in the small-group conversation or in the plenary synthesis, and say which you chose.

If you mix, give each group a written task, translated, so at least the framing is unambiguous.

## Platform limits

- **Zoom**: translated captions only. For instruction this may be adequate; for role-play it is not.
- **Teams Interpreter**: 10 languages, requires **Microsoft 365 Copilot**, excludes external guests — relevant if training includes contractors or partners.
- **Google Meet**: one English-anchored pair per meeting, 90-minute cap. Most training sessions are longer than 90 minutes.

Also check what happens to the recording, since training is usually rewatched. Microsoft states that Teams recordings **capture only the original meeting audio and do not include interpretation audio**, and Google Meet does not translate recordings. If the recording is the durable artefact — and for training it usually is — a live-only translation buys less than it appears to.
