Most webinar problems in a second language are not translation problems. They are planning problems that only become visible on the day.

## Decide the model first

**Human interpreters.** Two per language pair for anything over 30 minutes, booked weeks ahead, and a platform with interpreter channels. Right for regulated content, keynote-level stakes, and anything where a mistranslation has legal weight.

**AI live translation.** No booking, no minimum, works for a session announced on Tuesday for Thursday. The trade-off is that nobody is exercising judgement over ambiguity — an AI will translate a joke literally.

**Subtitles only.** Cheapest, and often enough. If your audience reads the language but struggles to follow spoken pace, captions solve the actual problem.

Be honest about which one you need. A 40-person internal product update does not need interpreters.

## Then check what your platform actually does

This is where plans fall apart, because "our platform supports translation" usually means something narrower than assumed:

- **Zoom** provides **translated captions only — no translated audio**. Your audience reads. It covers 34 languages plus 3 target-only, and needs Business Plus, Enterprise, or the Translated Captions add-on. Zoom's marketing page and its support documentation disagree on both the language count and what is included, so verify against your own plan rather than the pricing page.
- **Teams Interpreter** does voice-to-voice, but in **10 languages**, and it requires **Microsoft 365 Copilot** — not Teams Premium. It is also unavailable in webinars and town halls, which is usually the exact thing you were trying to do.
- **Google Meet** does voice-to-voice well but supports **one language pair per meeting**, English-anchored, with a 90-minute cap.

If your webinar is one language into one other language, under 90 minutes, and everyone has a licence, the native tooling may genuinely be enough. Use it.

## Build the run of show around the delay

Every model adds latency. Interpreters lag by a sentence, AI by a shorter but non-zero interval. That changes how you run the session:

1. **Brief speakers to pause at clause boundaries**, not mid-sentence. A speaker who trails off leaves the translation with nothing to close on.
2. **Put polls and Q&A prompts on screen as text**, not only spoken. Text does not have to wait.
3. **Slow the slide advance.** A slide that changes while the translation of the previous point is still arriving loses the audience twice.
4. **Rehearse with the translation running.** Not a dry run in one language — the timing problems only appear when the delay is there.

## Handle Q&A deliberately

Q&A is where multilingual webinars break. Audience questions arrive in a language the speaker does not have, often typed rather than spoken, often long.

Collect questions in writing throughout the session rather than opening a live mic at the end. It removes the turn-taking problem entirely, gives you time to translate accurately, and lets you group duplicate questions instead of answering the same one three times in three languages.

## Decide what happens to the recording

Ask this before the session, not after.

Microsoft's own documentation states that Teams **"meeting recordings only capture the original meeting audio and don't include interpretation audio"**, and translated transcripts disappear when the meeting ends. Google Meet does not translate recordings or live streams either.

For a live event this may not matter. For an all-hands that half the company will watch asynchronously across time zones, it means the translation you paid for serves the smaller half of your audience. If the recording matters, confirm the platform keeps the translated track before you choose it.

## A short pre-flight list

- Model chosen, and someone owns it
- Platform limits verified against your actual licence, not the marketing page
- Speakers briefed on pacing
- Q&A collected in writing
- Recording behaviour confirmed
- One rehearsal with translation live
