Distributed teams usually reach for a translation tool first. It is rarely the cheapest improvement available, and it works far better once the free changes are in place.

## Start with the asynchronous fixes

These cost nothing and remove more friction than live translation does.

**Decisions in writing, always.** Every decision, with owner and date, in a searchable place. Second-language readers can re-read at their own pace; nobody has to reconstruct a call from memory across a time-zone gap.

**Translate the channels.** Most team chat platforms translate messages in place. Turning it on lets people write in the language they think in, which raises both the quality and the quantity of what they contribute.

**Record meetings with transcripts.** A transcript is searchable, skimmable and translatable. It also fixes the time-zone problem and the language problem at once, which is why it is usually the highest-return single change.

**Write agendas and pre-reads.** Someone reading in a second language who has seen the material beforehand participates completely differently.

## Then decide which meetings need live translation

Not all of them. Sort your recurring meetings into two piles:

**Information transfer** — status, updates, announcements. These should mostly not be meetings. Written, translated, asynchronous.

**Discussion and decision** — design debates, retrospectives, planning, anything contested. These genuinely need everyone able to contribute in real time, and this is where live translation earns its cost.

Adding live translation to the first pile makes slow meetings slower. Adding it to the second changes who gets to influence outcomes.

## The thing nobody plans for

Language barriers in remote teams show up as **judgements about competence**. Someone who writes short, blunt messages in their fourth language gets read as terse or disengaged. Someone who is quiet on calls gets read as having no opinion.

This is worth naming with the team directly, because no tool addresses it. The practical countermeasure is to create channels where contribution does not depend on real-time second-language fluency — written proposals, async design review, explicit invitations by name.

## Platform limits, briefly

If you are evaluating live translation, check these before anything else:

- **Google Meet**: one language pair per meeting, English-anchored, 90-minute cap. A three-language team is out.
- **Teams Interpreter**: 10 languages, requires **Microsoft 365 Copilot** rather than Teams Premium, excludes external guests, unavailable in ad hoc meetings.
- **Zoom**: translated captions only — no translated audio.

And check what happens to recordings, because for a distributed team the recording is often more important than the live session. Microsoft's documentation states that Teams recordings **capture only the original meeting audio and do not include interpretation audio**, with translated transcripts disappearing when the meeting ends. Google Meet does not translate recordings or live streams either.

For a team spread across time zones, that limitation removes most of the value. Worth knowing before you choose.

## A reasonable order of operations

1. Decisions in writing
2. Channel translation on
3. Recording with transcripts
4. Agendas and pre-reads
5. Cut the status meetings
6. **Then** evaluate live translation, for discussion meetings only
