"Adding interpretation" means two very different projects depending on which route you take. Pick the route first.

## Route one: human interpreters

Still the right answer for anything where a mistranslation carries legal, financial or safety weight.

**What you need to book:** two interpreters per language pair for any session over 30 minutes. Simultaneous interpreting is cognitively brutal and interpreters swap every 15-20 minutes. One interpreter for a 90-minute webinar is not a saving, it is a quality decision you have made by accident.

**Lead time:** two weeks minimum for common pairs, longer for rare ones or specialist domains. KUDO's marketplace can move faster — around two hours' notice against 12,000 interpreters — but that is the exception, not the norm.

**Brief them properly.** This is the step most often skipped and it is the one that determines quality:

- Slides, in advance, in final form
- A glossary of product names, internal acronyms and anything that must not be translated
- Speaker names with pronunciation
- The run of show, so they know when to expect a handover
- Any pre-recorded segments, so they can prepare rather than sight-translate

**Platform requirement:** you need interpreter channels — separate audio streams the audience selects. Not every plan has this. Confirm before you book people.

## Route two: AI live translation

No booking, no minimum, works when the session was announced on Tuesday for Thursday.

The honest trade-off is judgement. An interpreter hears an ambiguous phrase and resolves it from context, or asks. An AI translates it literally. For a product update that is fine. For a works council consultation it is not.

Setup is shorter but not zero:

1. **Load a glossary** if the tool supports one — product names, acronyms, anything domain-specific. This is the single highest-leverage step.
2. **Test with a real speaker**, not with someone reading a script. Read speech and spoken speech stress the system differently.
3. **Decide the fallback.** If the translation fails mid-session, what happens? Usually: switch to captions, or continue in the source language and circulate a translated transcript afterwards. Decide now, not live.

## Route three: captions only

Worth considering seriously rather than treating as the consolation prize. If your audience reads the language competently but struggles with spoken pace, accent or jargon, captions solve the real problem at a fraction of the cost — and they help the native speakers too.

## What your platform will let you do

- **Zoom** supports translated captions only. There is no translated audio channel, so route one needs an external interpreting platform alongside it.
- **Teams Interpreter** does voice-to-voice in 10 languages, but is **not available in webinars or town halls**, and needs Microsoft 365 Copilot. For a webinar specifically, this is usually a dead end.
- **Google Meet** does voice-to-voice but one language pair per meeting, English-anchored, capped at 90 minutes.

If none of that fits, you are looking at a dedicated event platform or an AI tool that runs alongside your conferencing.

## Before the session

- Interpreters confirmed and briefed, or AI tested with a real speaker
- Audience told how to select their language, in their language
- A rehearsal with interpretation actually running
- A decision recorded about what the recording will contain
