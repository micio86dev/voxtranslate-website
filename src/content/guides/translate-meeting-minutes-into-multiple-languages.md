Translated minutes fail far more often because of how they were written than because of how they were translated. Fixing the source is cheaper and helps the original-language readers too.

## Write for translation

**Decisions, not narrative.** "The team discussed several approaches and there was broad support for the second option" translates into five languages as five slightly different degrees of commitment. "Decision: adopt option B. Owner: Marta. Date: 14 September" translates identically every time.

**One idea per sentence.** Long sentences with embedded qualifications are where translation drifts, and where a reader in their second language loses the thread.

**Name people and dates explicitly.** "He will handle it next week" is ambiguous in the original and worse after translation. So is "by end of month" across time zones.

**Separate decisions, actions and discussion.** Three headings. Most readers only need the first two, and in a second language that saves real effort.

**List terms that must not be translated** — product names, internal systems, legal entities. Keep this list and reuse it; it improves every subsequent set of minutes.

## Then translate

Once the source is clean, the mechanics are simple. Two decisions worth making deliberately:

**Which languages?** Not automatically everyone's. Ask. Some people would rather read the original than a translation they distrust, particularly for technical content.

**Does a human review it?** For routine internal minutes, usually no. For anything with contractual, financial or compliance weight, yes — and the reviewer should be someone who was in the meeting, not only a translator, because the errors that matter are usually about meaning rather than language.

## Mark the authoritative version

State clearly which language version governs if they ever conflict. One line at the top.

This costs nothing and prevents the specific dispute where two parties are each holding a version that supports their reading.

## Distribute all versions together

Send every language at the same time, in the same message or the same folder. Staggered distribution — the English version now, translations on Thursday — tells people whose reading matters and reliably means the translations get ignored, because the decisions have already been acted on.

## If you generate minutes from a transcript

Increasingly common, and it works, with two cautions.

**A transcript is not minutes.** It is raw material. Someone still has to decide what was actually decided, and a summary that hedges because the conversation hedged is not useful in any language.

**Check what the recording actually captured.** If you rely on a platform's recording to produce minutes for a translated meeting, note that Microsoft's documentation states Teams **recordings capture only the original meeting audio and do not include interpretation audio**, with translated transcripts disappearing when the meeting ends. Google Meet does not translate recordings either.

You will usually be working from the original-language transcript and translating afterwards — which is fine, and often better, but worth knowing rather than discovering.
