Support has a constraint other call types do not: the customer is already unhappy, and asking them to also operate in a second language compounds it.

## Route first, translate second

If you have a native speaker available and the issue is non-trivial, route to them. Live translation is the answer when routing is not possible — not a replacement for a support team that covers your customers' languages.

Where routing usually beats translation:

- **Emotionally charged calls.** Complaints, escalations, cancellations. Tone carries most of the resolution and it is the first thing lost in translation.
- **Long diagnostic conversations**, where accumulated small ambiguities compound.
- **Anything with a commercial outcome** — refunds, credits, contract changes.

Where translation is fine or better:

- **Short, factual issues** — status, how-to, configuration
- **Out-of-hours coverage**, where the alternative is nobody
- **Long-tail languages** you will never staff

## Set it up properly

**Load a glossary** of product names, feature names, error codes and plan names. Support vocabulary is proper nouns and jargon, which is exactly where general translation fails and where a wrong rendering sends the customer to the wrong place.

**Tell the customer translation is running**, briefly and without ceremony. It sets expectations for the pause and lets them slow down.

**Never translate error codes or identifiers.** Put them in the chat, in the original.

## During the call

- **Confirm the problem by restatement** before proposing a fix. Half of support failures across a language barrier are solving the wrong problem confidently.
- **One step at a time.** Multi-step instructions given in a burst do not survive translation or stress.
- **Use the chat for anything to be typed or clicked.** Menu paths, URLs, settings names.
- **Check after each step**, not at the end.

## Always follow up in writing

The single highest-return practice in multilingual support: send the resolution in writing, in the customer's language, before you close.

It gives them something to re-read and act on later, it reduces repeat contacts, and it protects both sides if the verbal exchange was ambiguous. It also means a customer who did not fully follow the call is not left guessing — they will not say so on the phone.

## Platform note

Support calls are external and often ad hoc, which is the hardest case for native tooling.

**Teams Interpreter excludes external guests and anonymous users**, requires **Microsoft 365 Copilot**, and is unavailable in ad hoc meetings — three separate disqualifications for inbound support. **Google Meet** does one English-anchored language pair per meeting. **Zoom** offers translated captions only, which is awkward when the customer is following instructions on the same screen.

## A note on measurement

If you introduce translation in support, watch **repeat contact rate** rather than handle time. Translation lengthens calls, and that is not a failure — a longer call that resolves the issue is cheaper than a short one that does not.
